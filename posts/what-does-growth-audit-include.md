## What a growth audit includes, in short

A growth audit is a structured review of how your business finds customers and turns them into revenue. It should cover your tracking, your paid ads, your search visibility, your website, and what happens to a lead after they contact you. The output is a ranked list of fixes with estimated costs and likely impact.

Pricing varies widely. Published guides put outside marketing audits at roughly **$500 to $10,000**. MarginFix's 2026 cost guide gives that range for small businesses. Ryan Spelts Marketing says comprehensive audits for small and mid-sized businesses typically run **$3,000 to $10,000**, and that cheaper ones tend to stay surface-level. Many agencies run a lighter version free or at low cost as a way to start a conversation, so ask what you get and what happens afterwards.

## The six areas a proper audit should cover

Audits differ, but a useful one for a local or service business checks these areas. If one is missing, ask why.

- **Measurement:** can you tell which leads came from which source?
- **Paid ads:** Google, Meta and Instagram account structure, spend and results.
- **Local and organic search:** Google Business Profile, reviews, and rankings for the searches your customers use.
- **Website and landing pages:** speed, mobile use, and how easy it is to call or book.
- **Lead handling:** how fast and how well enquiries get answered.
- **Offer and economics:** what a customer is worth and what you can afford to pay for one.

The last item is the one cheap audits skip. Without it, nobody can say whether a $90 cost per lead is good or ruinous.

## Measurement: the first thing to check

Most audits should start with tracking, because every other finding depends on it. If your call tracking, form tracking and ad conversion tags are wrong, the ad reports you have been reading are wrong too.

The auditor should check whether Google Ads conversions count real enquiries, such as calls over a set length and completed forms, rather than page views or button clicks. They should also check whether phone calls are attributed to a source and whether you can see which leads became paying customers.

A common finding is that a business is paying for ads and cannot say which of its last ten new customers came from them. That is a measurement problem, and it is worth fixing before changing anything else.

## Paid ads review: Google, Meta and Instagram

If you run ads, the auditor should look inside the accounts, not just at a summary report. You should expect them to review:

- **Spend against results:** cost per lead and, where possible, cost per customer, by campaign.
- **Search terms:** the actual searches that triggered your ads. Wasted spend on irrelevant terms is one of the most common findings.
- **Targeting and location settings:** are you paying to reach people outside your service area?
- **Ad copy and creative:** whether ads match what people searched for and what you actually offer.
- **Landing pages:** where the click goes and whether it makes the next step obvious.
- **Account access and ownership:** the ad accounts should be in your name, not your previous agency's.

On the last point, check it yourself first. If you cannot log in as an administrator, that is a finding in its own right.

For a view of what changes after this kind of work, Zapplon publishes [client results from its growth work](https://www.zapplon.com/results). Treat any agency case study as a starting point and ask how comparable the business was to yours.

## Local SEO and online reputation

For a business that serves customers in one area, local search often produces the cheapest leads. The audit should look at your Google Business Profile: whether categories, services, hours and photos are complete and accurate, and whether the business name, address and phone number match across the web.

Reviews matter here. The auditor should count your reviews, note how recent they are, check whether you reply, and compare you with the three or four competitors who show up above you in the map results.

They should also check rankings for a short list of real searches, such as "emergency plumber near me" or "med spa in Sacramento", from locations where your customers actually are. Ranking for your own business name does not count.

## Website and landing pages

You do not need a website redesign to get more leads. You need to know if the site is losing people at an obvious point. A good audit checks the site on a phone first, since that is where most local searches happen.

Look for findings such as:

- Page load times that are slow on mobile.
- A phone number that cannot be tapped to call.
- Contact forms that are long or break on certain devices.
- No clear page for each service you sell.
- Pricing, service area or booking details that are hard to find.

For a Shopify store, the same review would cover product pages, checkout steps, and where shoppers drop out. Ask the auditor to show the evidence, such as screenshots or analytics data, rather than a generic score out of 100.

## Lead handling: where many businesses lose the most

This is the part of a growth audit that business owners often find most useful, and many audits ignore it. Generating a lead costs money. Missing it wastes that money.

A thorough audit tests your process. The auditor calls your number at different times, including evenings and weekends, submits your web form, and notes how long a reply takes and what the experience is like. They should look at how many calls go to voicemail and how long a missed enquiry waits for a follow-up.

For a dental office, contractor or salon, the finding is often straightforward: calls come in while staff are with customers, and nobody calls back for hours. In that case, the fix may be a better phone process, not more ads. Depending on volume, that could be a part-time hire, a call-answering service or an AI receptionist. The right one depends on call volume and how complex your calls are.

## What a good audit delivers

The report itself matters as much as the review. A useful audit gives you:

- **A short summary** in plain English that you can read in ten minutes.
- **Specific findings with evidence**, not generic advice.
- **A ranked action list** showing what to fix first, who should do it and roughly what it costs.
- **Estimated impact**, stated as a range with its assumptions, not a promise.
- **A walkthrough call** where you can ask questions.
- **Ownership of the work:** the report and any access, tags or accounts created are yours.

If the report is mostly a long list of metrics with no priorities, you have bought data, not an audit.

## How to tell a real audit from a sales pitch

Many free audits are lead-generation tools. That is not necessarily bad, but you should know what you are getting. A free audit that looks only at what the agency sells will nearly always conclude you need it.

Warning signs include:

- **An automated report** produced in minutes with no one looking at your accounts.
- **Scare numbers** with no explanation of how they were calculated.
- **No questions about your business**, such as margins, capacity, average job value and seasonality.
- **A guaranteed result** before anyone has seen your data.
- **Pressure to sign a long contract** right after the audit.

A better sign is an auditor who tells you what not to spend money on. If the honest finding is that your Google Business Profile needs a weekend of cleanup and your ads are fine, a good auditor says so.

## When you probably do not need a paid audit

A paid audit makes sense when you spend meaningful money on marketing and cannot tell what is working. If you spend under about $1,000 a month on ads, a $5,000 audit is out of proportion. Take a smaller step first.

Cheaper checks you can do yourself:

- Log in to your ad accounts and look at the search terms report.
- Call your own business after hours and see what happens.
- Search for your top three services in your area, on your phone.
- Ask your last ten customers how they found you.

If you have just started marketing and have no data, an audit has little to review. Run something for a few months first.

## Questions to ask before you buy an audit

Ask these before you commit:

1. Who will do the work, and have they managed accounts like mine?
2. Which tools and data will you use, and will you need access to my accounts?
3. What exactly is in the report, and can I see a sample?
4. Is the fee credited against future work, and am I free to take the report elsewhere?
5. How long will it take? A thorough audit generally takes days to a couple of weeks, not an hour.

## FAQ

### How much does a growth audit cost?

Published ranges for outside marketing audits run from about $500 to $10,000, according to MarginFix. Ryan Spelts Marketing puts comprehensive audits for small and mid-sized firms at $3,000 to $10,000. Scope drives the price: a single-channel review costs less than one covering ads, SEO, tracking and lead handling together.

### How long does a growth audit take?

A focused audit for a small business usually takes several days to two weeks, depending on how quickly you can provide account access. Anything delivered within minutes is likely an automated scan, not a review by a person.

### Is a growth audit different from a marketing audit?

The terms overlap heavily. A growth audit tends to put more weight on revenue: lead volume, cost per customer and conversion from enquiry to sale. A traditional marketing audit may spend more time on brand and messaging. Check the deliverables list rather than relying on the name.

## What to do next

Before you pay for anything, gather your last three months of ad spend, your lead counts by source and your average sale value. With those numbers, even a basic audit becomes far more useful, and you will spot a vague one quickly. If you want an outside view, ask for a sample report first and confirm the audit will include tracking and lead handling.
