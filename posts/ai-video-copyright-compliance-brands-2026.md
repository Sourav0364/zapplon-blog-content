## Why AI video copyright compliance matters now

Generative video can help a marketing team turn a product idea into a storyboard, social clip, or campaign variation quickly. But speed does not remove the need to check rights. A brand may need to consider the ownership of its inputs, the identity of people or characters shown, the music and voice used, and the terms of the AI video service that created the output.

The issue is receiving fresh attention after Hollywood’s Motion Picture Association (MPA) and ByteDance announced a global memorandum of understanding focused on intellectual-property protections for ByteDance’s generative AI video and image models. The framework covers models including Seedance and Seedream, which are offered across products such as TikTok, CapCut, and Dreamina. Marketing-Interactive reported that the agreement is intended to maintain guardrails and continue cooperation as the technology evolves.

The agreement follows earlier tensions. The MPA sent ByteDance a cease-and-desist letter in February concerning Seedream 5.0 Lite and Seedance 2.0, after which the organizations discussed protections for film and television intellectual property. The MPA and ByteDance said newer releases, including Seedream 5.0 Pro and Seedance 2.5, include further advances in IP protections.

This development does **not** mean that every output from an AI video tool is automatically cleared for advertising. It does show why brands need an internal process for **AI video copyright compliance** before publishing or spending media budget behind a generated asset.

## What copyright compliance covers in an AI video

Copyright is only one part of the rights picture. A useful review should examine at least five layers:

- **Input assets:** product photography, footage, illustrations, logos, music, scripts, and reference images supplied to the tool.
- **Generated elements:** backgrounds, characters, scenes, dialogue, music, sound effects, and visual styles created by the model.
- **People and likeness:** recognizable employees, creators, celebrities, customers, or fictional characters.
- **Brand and third-party marks:** names, packaging, logos, uniforms, locations, and designs that could imply an association.
- **Distribution and use:** paid ads, organic social posts, websites, retail screens, television, and markets in which the video will appear.

The precise legal position can vary by jurisdiction and by the facts of a particular work. A brand should not assume that an AI provider’s commercial-use language settles every question. Provider terms, platform policies, contracts, and local law all deserve review.

A simple rule is to separate **permission to use the input** from **permission to publish the output**. Owning a product photo may allow a company to provide that photo to a production partner, but it does not necessarily authorize the addition of a third party’s music, a competitor’s trademark, or a recognizable person’s likeness.

## Build an approved asset library first

The safest AI video workflow starts with assets the brand can identify and control. Create a library that records the source, owner, license, permitted channels, territory, expiration date, and any restrictions for each asset.

A practical library may include:

- Original product photos and video captured by the brand or its production partner.
- Logos and brand guidelines with approved placement and color rules.
- Music and sound-effect licenses that explicitly cover advertising and the intended platforms.
- Model and location releases for people and places appearing in source footage.
- Approved claims, product descriptions, disclaimers, and mandatory disclosures.
- A list of prohibited references, such as protected characters or competitor branding.

Tag each asset with a clear status such as **approved**, **needs review**, **expired**, or **do not use**. Keep the license record with the file rather than in a separate spreadsheet that can become disconnected from the production process.

This approach also improves creative consistency. When a video workflow is built around approved product angles, logos, colors, and language, the team spends less time correcting avoidable errors after generation.

## Use a rights-aware prompt and production brief

A prompt is not a legal clearance document, but a well-designed brief can reduce risk. Before generation, state what the model may use and what it must avoid. Describe the commercial purpose, target audience, distribution channels, and intended territory.

For example, a brief for an ecommerce product video might specify:

- Use only the supplied product images and approved packaging.
- Create an original setting rather than reproducing a recognizable film scene.
- Do not include celebrity likenesses, fictional characters, or third-party logos.
- Use an approved voice or a newly commissioned voice, with written permission.
- Use music from the brand’s cleared library or commission original audio.
- Do not make health, performance, or environmental claims outside the approved copy.
- Mark any required AI or advertising disclosure before publication.

Keep the brief, prompt versions, reference files, tool name, model version if available, and generated outputs in a project record. That documentation helps a team understand how an asset was made and makes it easier to respond to a platform, client, or rights-holder question.

## Add human review before an AI video ad goes live

Automation is valuable for producing variations, but publication should have a clear approval gate. A reviewer should watch the complete video—not only the prompt or the first frame—and compare it with the brief and the asset register.

The review can follow this checklist:

1. **Source check:** Are all supplied images, footage, music, voices, and text approved for this use?
2. **Output check:** Does the video contain an unexpected logo, character, face, song, slogan, or recognizable location?
3. **Claims check:** Are product claims accurate, supported, and consistent with the approved copy?
4. **Disclosure check:** Are platform, market, and campaign disclosure requirements addressed?
5. **Brand check:** Are the product, logo, colors, and tone represented correctly?
6. **Accessibility check:** Are captions accurate, readable, and synchronized? Is important information available without sound?
7. **Archive check:** Are the final file and review decision stored with the relevant rights records?

Set different review levels for different use cases. A low-risk internal concept may need a creative review. A paid public campaign using a person’s likeness, regulated product claims, or third-party material may require legal or specialist review before launch.

## Manage AI video copyright risk across the campaign lifecycle

Compliance is not finished when the file is exported. A campaign may create new versions for different aspect ratios, languages, audiences, and platforms. Each variation should inherit the original asset record and be checked for changes.

Maintain version control for:

- The original generated file and every edited version.
- Captions, translations, voiceovers, and music substitutions.
- Crops that may remove a disclaimer or alter the product presentation.
- Platform-specific edits and thumbnails.
- Approval dates, reviewers, and any conditions attached to approval.
- Takedown, replacement, and expiration procedures.

Also monitor the tools in use. A provider may change model capabilities, terms, content safeguards, or commercial-use conditions. Before a major campaign, confirm the current terms and keep a copy of the relevant policy or contract in the project record.

For agencies and service providers, contracts should clarify who supplies the assets, who approves the output, who owns or licenses the final work, and who responds if a rights-holder raises an issue. Clear responsibility is more useful than assuming that “AI-generated” means nobody is accountable.

## Turn compliance into a creative advantage

Rights review does not have to make AI video slow or generic. It can give creative teams a repeatable system for exploring ideas safely. When the team knows which assets, voices, claims, and visual directions are approved, it can generate more variations within a defined boundary.

A rights-aware system also protects the brand from avoidable distractions. An unexpected protected character, unlicensed song, or misleading visual can turn a performance campaign into a customer-service, platform, or legal problem. A short review before launch is usually easier than replacing an ad after it has been distributed.

Use AI video where it provides a clear benefit, such as:

- Creating product explainers from approved images and copy.
- Testing different hooks and opening scenes for paid social.
- Localizing a script with approved translations and voice talent.
- Producing multiple aspect ratios from one reviewed concept.
- Visualizing a campaign idea before commissioning a full production.

Keep a person responsible for the final decision. Generative tools can accelerate production and ideation, but brands still need judgment about rights, accuracy, audience expectations, and reputation.

## A practical 2026 workflow for brands

A small team can implement AI video copyright compliance without building a large legal operation. Start with a single campaign and create five repeatable stages:

1. **Plan:** Define the objective, channels, territory, claims, and risk level.
2. **Source:** Select only assets with documented permission and approved usage.
3. **Generate:** Use a tool whose terms and safeguards fit the campaign, while retaining prompts and outputs.
4. **Review:** Check rights, claims, brand standards, accessibility, platform rules, and disclosures.
5. **Publish and monitor:** Keep versions, watch for issues, and maintain a process for correction or removal.

After the first campaign, record the questions that slowed review. Turn those answers into templates, approved prompt patterns, and reusable checklists. This makes the next production cycle faster without treating compliance as an afterthought.

The MPA–ByteDance memorandum is a reminder that standards and safeguards will continue to develop as generative video becomes more capable. Brands that build their own documented process can adapt more easily than teams that rely on informal assumptions.

## FAQ: AI video copyright compliance

### Can a brand use any video made with an AI generator in an advertisement?

No. The brand should review the tool’s terms, input permissions, output, third-party elements, likeness rights, claims, platform rules, and the laws that apply to the campaign.

### Does the MPA–ByteDance agreement clear all Seedance or Seedream content?

No. The agreement is a memorandum of understanding establishing a framework for continued cooperation and safeguards. It is not a blanket clearance for every generated asset or every commercial use.

### What should be documented for an AI video campaign?

Keep the asset licenses, releases, prompts, tool and model information where available, generated files, edits, claims review, approval decision, and distribution details.

### Should AI-generated video always be labeled?

Disclosure requirements can depend on the platform, country, type of content, and campaign. Check the applicable rules and policies, and use clear disclosure when it is required or appropriate.

### Who is responsible for the final ad?

The brand and its contracted partners should define responsibility in advance. A human reviewer should approve the final asset and confirm that the campaign meets rights, brand, accuracy, and platform requirements.
