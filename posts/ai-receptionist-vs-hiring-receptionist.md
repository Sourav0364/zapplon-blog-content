## The short answer: what each option costs

A full-time in-house receptionist typically costs **$50,000 to $55,000 a year** once you add payroll taxes, insurance and paid leave. An AI receptionist typically costs **$25 to $300 a month** ($300 to $3,600 a year), and hybrid services that add live humans run **$300 to $2,000+ a month**.

That is a gap of roughly 15 to 150 times in price. But price is not the whole decision. A person can greet a patient at the door, calm an upset customer and handle a request nobody planned for. An AI cannot do those things.

The right answer for most small offices is one of three setups: AI alone, AI plus a part-time person, or a full-time hire. The sections below show you which fits your call volume and budget.

## What a human receptionist really costs

Start with the wage. The Bureau of Labor Statistics reports a median hourly wage for receptionists of $17.90 in May 2024. The wage is only part of the bill.

BLS data for private employers in June 2026 shows wages and salaries made up 70.0 percent of employer compensation costs, with benefits making up the other 30.0 percent. Benefits here include paid leave, insurance, retirement contributions and legally required items such as payroll taxes and workers' compensation.

Here is a rough calculation using those figures. It is an estimate, not a quote:

- $17.90 an hour x 2,080 hours = about **$37,200** in wages
- Add roughly 30% of total cost for benefits and taxes = about **$53,000** all-in

That lines up with other published estimates. One provider guide puts an in-house receptionist at about $53,700 a year.

In California, the floor is higher. The statewide minimum wage rose to $16.90 an hour on January 1, 2026, and California's Director of Finance has certified an increase to $17.40 on January 1, 2027. Many cities set their own higher rates, so a front-desk hire in San Francisco, San Jose or Los Angeles will often cost more than the national median.

Then add the costs that never show up in a salary figure:

- **Recruiting and training:** job ads, interview time and several weeks before the person is fully useful.
- **Cover for absences:** holidays, sick days and lunch breaks leave the phone unanswered.
- **Equipment and desk space:** a computer, phone line and software licences.
- **Turnover:** if the person leaves, you pay to recruit and train again.

## What an AI receptionist really costs

AI receptionists are priced in three main ways: a flat monthly fee, per call, or per minute. One comparison guide lists per-call pricing from $0.75 to $2.40 and per-minute pricing from $0.25 to $0.48, with small-business plans from $25 to $300 a month. Treat these as published ranges from vendors and comparison sites, since prices change often.

Smith.ai's hybrid AI-plus-human plan starts at $292.50 a month, and Ruby's live receptionists start at $235 a month, according to one comparison guide. Those are useful benchmarks if you want a human voice on some calls.

What drives the price up or down:

- **Call volume.** A dental office taking 400 calls a month pays far more than a solo plumber taking 40.
- **Call length.** Per-minute plans punish long, complicated calls.
- **Integrations.** Connecting to your booking software, CRM or calendar often costs extra, or needs a higher plan.
- **Custom setup.** A tailored voice agent built around your services, pricing rules and escalation steps costs more than a template. You can see what that involves in this overview of [AI voice agents and AI receptionists for small businesses](https://www.zapplon.com/ai-agents).
- **Human back-up.** Hybrid plans cost several times more than AI-only plans.

Watch for overage charges. A plan with 30 included calls looks cheap until you have a busy month.

## What a human does better

An AI receptionist is a good phone answerer. It is not a replacement for everything a front-desk person does. Be honest with yourself about this part of your job.

A human is clearly better when:

- **Visitors walk in.** Someone has to greet clients, take payments, hand over paperwork and manage the waiting room.
- **Callers are upset or vulnerable.** A frightened patient or an angry customer with a billing dispute needs empathy and judgement.
- **Requests are unusual.** People ask odd questions. A good receptionist knows when to bend a rule and when not to.
- **Work extends past the phone.** Filing, insurance verification, ordering supplies and supporting staff are all part of many front-desk roles.
- **Accuracy is critical.** In regulated settings such as healthcare and legal intake, an error carries real risk.

If your receptionist spends most of the day on tasks other than the phone, an AI will not remove the need for that person. It may reduce the load, which can mean fewer hours rather than no hire.

## What an AI does better

AI wins on a narrower set of jobs, but they are the ones that cost small businesses money.

- **Never-missed calls.** It answers on the first ring, at any hour, including when you are in the middle of a job or a procedure.
- **No queue.** It can take several calls at the same time. A human receptionist cannot.
- **Consistent answers.** Hours, directions, service areas and pricing ranges are given the same way each time.
- **Instant booking and capture.** It can book into your calendar, take a name and number, and send you a text summary.
- **Predictable cost.** The bill scales with calls, not with salary reviews or sick leave.

The business case is usually about missed revenue, not saved wages. A contractor who misses a $4,000 job call because they were on a roof has lost more than a month of AI fees in one call. Track how many calls you miss now. Your phone system's call log will show it, and that number is the best input to this decision.

## Limits and risks to check before you buy

AI receptionists have real weaknesses. Test them before you commit.

- **Accents, noise and crosstalk.** Speech recognition can stumble on a bad line, a heavy accent or a caller in a car. Ask for a trial and listen to real recordings.
- **Wrong answers.** If the AI is not given accurate information, it may guess. Insist on a setup where it says it does not know and passes the caller on.
- **Caller reaction.** Some customers dislike talking to a machine. Decide whether to disclose that it is AI, and check your state's rules. Some places have disclosure requirements for automated calls.
- **Emergencies.** Set up clear rules for urgent calls, such as a burst pipe or a medical symptom, so they reach a person immediately.
- **Privacy and compliance.** If you take health or legal information on calls, you need to ask where recordings are stored and who can access them.
- **Contract terms.** Check cancellation terms, overage rates and whether your phone number stays with you if you leave.

A cheap plan with poor call handling can cost you customers. A $30 a month tool that frustrates callers is not a saving.

## How to decide which option fits you

Work through these questions in order.

1. **How many calls do you get a month, and how many do you miss?** Under about 100 calls with a high miss rate points to AI-only. Several hundred calls a month with complex needs points to a person or a hybrid.
2. **Do people physically come to your door?** If yes, you need a human at the front, at least part of the week. AI can then cover overflow, lunch breaks and after hours.
3. **How complex are the calls?** Booking, hours and basic questions suit AI. Negotiation, triage and sensitive advice do not.
4. **What is a missed call worth?** Multiply your average job or appointment value by your close rate. That number sets how much it is worth paying to answer every call.
5. **What is your budget?** If a $50,000 hire is out of reach, AI plus a part-time admin is often the practical middle path.

Common setups that work:

- **Solo or two-person trades business:** AI-only, with urgent calls forwarded to your mobile.
- **Salon, clinic or dental office with a front desk:** keep the person, add AI for after hours, lunch and overflow.
- **Busy office with a full-time receptionist who is overloaded:** add AI to take routine calls so the person can focus on the people in front of them.

Run a 30-day trial before cutting any headcount. Compare booked appointments, missed calls and customer complaints against the previous month.

## FAQ

### Is an AI receptionist cheaper than hiring a receptionist?

Yes, in direct cost. AI-only plans generally run $25 to $300 a month, against roughly $50,000 or more a year for a full-time hire including benefits. The comparison is only fair if the AI can do the work you need. If your receptionist handles walk-ins and paperwork, AI will reduce the workload but not remove the role.

### Can an AI receptionist fully replace a human?

For phone-only work such as answering, booking and taking messages, often yes. For in-person reception, sensitive conversations and work beyond the phone, no. Many small businesses use both: AI to cover every call, and a person for everything that needs a human.

### How long does it take to set up an AI receptionist?

A template-based plan can be live in days. A custom setup with calendar and CRM integration, plus testing on real calls, can take a few weeks. Hiring a person usually takes longer once you count recruiting, notice periods and training.

## The bottom line

If you only need the phone answered, AI is far cheaper and covers every hour of the week. If you need a face at the front desk, keep a person and use AI to fill the gaps. In either case, count your missed calls first. That number tells you what the problem is costing you today.
