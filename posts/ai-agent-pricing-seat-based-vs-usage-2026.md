## Why AI agent pricing is getting a closer look

A question raised in a [new CDOTrends article](https://www.cdotrends.com/story/5158/four-seats-90-seconds-work-why-ai-agents-break-software-bill) captures a practical challenge for businesses evaluating AI agents: if one agent starts a task, then creates three short-lived helper agents, should the software bill count one seat or four? The article describes an example from Bem CEO Antonio Bustamante, whose company processes documents into structured data. In the example, a document-checking agent delegates data extraction, compliance checking, and record cross-checking to three helpers, each of which exists for about 90 seconds. Bem charges by function call, according to the report.

The example is a useful prompt for procurement—not proof that every agent product uses the same architecture or billing method. It points to a broader buying question: does the pricing model match the way your team will actually use the system? For **AI agent pricing**, comparing only a monthly license can miss the cost of usage, supervision, exceptions, and work that still requires a person.

## Seat-based pricing: familiar, but check what counts as a seat

A seat-based model charges for a licensed user or account, typically over a billing period. It can be easy to understand when a person uses a defined software workspace. But autonomous or delegated agents may not map neatly to a human user's work pattern. Some agents may run only for a short task; others may be persistent or shared across processes.

Before accepting a quote, ask the vendor to define the billable unit in writing:

- Does each agent identity require a paid seat?
- Are temporary helper agents counted separately?
- Can multiple workflows or people use one agent account?
- Is a human supervisor also charged as a seat?
- Are testing, staging, or inactive agents billable?

Do not assume the seat model is automatically wrong. A predictable subscription may make budgeting easier for a known scope. The key is to understand whether the license count reflects actual workload and what happens when agent use changes.

## Usage-based pricing: connect the meter to your workload

Usage-based pricing ties charges to a measurable unit, such as a function call or another defined operation. In the example reported by CDOTrends, Bem says it charges by function call. That can make short or variable tasks easier to represent than a full monthly seat, but a meter is useful only when the buyer understands what is being counted and can forecast the bill.

Ask questions such as:

- What event triggers a charge, and how is it counted?
- Are retries, failed attempts, or helper-agent calls included?
- Can the vendor show usage by workflow, team, or task?
- Is there a budget limit, usage alert, or approval control?
- What happens to charges when an agent repeats a task or calls an external tool?

Avoid comparing two vendors based only on their headline unit price. Define a representative task and estimate all the metered work needed to complete it, including retries and any billable components listed in the contract. Then compare that estimate with observed usage from a controlled pilot.

## Measure cost per completed outcome, not just activity

The number of seats or calls tells you how the vendor bills; it does not necessarily tell you whether the business received value. A more decision-useful comparison asks what it costs to produce a completed, acceptable result.

For a pilot, define one measurable outcome—for example, a document reviewed against a checklist or a qualified support request routed correctly. Agree on what counts as completion and what requires human review. Then track:

1. **Successful completions:** tasks meeting the business's quality criteria.
2. **Human effort:** review, correction, exception handling, and escalation.
3. **Agent activity:** billable calls, runs, or licenses required for the task.
4. **Operational overhead:** setup, monitoring, and upkeep required to keep the workflow usable.
5. **Failure impact:** what happens when the agent makes an error, duplicates work, or stops before completion.

Divide the relevant total cost by the number of outcomes that meet the agreed standard. This is an internal comparison method, not a universal vendor price metric. Keep the assumptions visible, and compare like with like: the same task, quality threshold, and scope of human oversight.

## Include control, auditability, and data readiness

A low apparent cost is not a complete buying case if the workflow cannot be supervised or its decisions cannot be examined. The CDOTrends article also highlights audit trails and the cost of a finished outcome as questions buyers should raise. Treat these as procurement checks alongside the price model.

Ask the vendor or implementation team to demonstrate:

- How a user can inspect the steps an agent took and the sources it used.
- Whether a person can pause, review, or approve consequential actions.
- How access to business data and connected systems is limited.
- What information is retained for debugging and audit purposes.
- How the workflow handles missing, conflicting, or inaccessible data.

Data quality also matters. In its interview, the article's subject argues that agents can be constrained by data they cannot use. For your own workflow, check whether relevant documents and records are accessible, current, and structured well enough for the task. An agent cannot reliably complete work from information it cannot reach or interpret; include data preparation and maintenance in the implementation estimate.

## A practical checklist for evaluating AI agent cost

Before signing a contract or expanding a deployment, use this checklist:

- **Map the work:** identify the task, its frequency, and the systems an agent needs.
- **Clarify the meter:** document billable seats, runs, calls, retries, and add-ons.
- **Pilot a representative task:** track actual usage and human effort rather than relying only on a demo.
- **Set a quality bar:** define completion, acceptable errors, and when a person must intervene.
- **Calculate cost per outcome:** include licenses or usage, oversight, and upkeep in a consistent comparison.
- **Review controls:** verify access limits, approvals, and audit evidence for the planned use.
- **Stress-test growth:** ask how the bill changes if volume rises or the workflow creates more helper tasks.

If possible, agree on a usage report and alert thresholds before expanding. Revisit the estimate when the workflow, model, data sources, or volume changes. Agent economics are tied to a specific process and configuration; a cost estimate for one workflow should not be treated as a guarantee for another.

## FAQ: AI agent pricing

### Is seat-based pricing always a bad fit for AI agents?

No. It may be predictable for a clearly defined scope. Ask how agent identities, temporary helpers, people, and testing environments are counted before comparing it with other models.

### Is usage-based pricing automatically cheaper?

No. The total depends on the vendor's billable units and the work required to complete a task. Estimate costs from a representative pilot, including retries and human review.

### What is a useful way to compare AI agent costs?

Compare the total cost of producing an outcome that meets agreed quality criteria. Include usage or licenses, human oversight, exceptions, and ongoing upkeep.

### What should businesses ask about agent billing?

Ask what triggers a charge, whether helper-agent activity or retries are included, how usage is reported, and what controls are available to limit unexpected spending.
