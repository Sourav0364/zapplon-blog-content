## Why AI agents for business purchasing are getting attention

An AI agent that can act across connected services raises a practical question for business owners: could it help with repeat purchasing tasks, and what must remain under human control? Interest has grown with new agent announcements. On October 21, 2026, Reuters reported that Anthropic had unveiled Claude Duet, a standalone agent app for professionals and small-business owners. Anthropic said it can handle tasks and, for business purchases, use Stripe's Link digital wallet; the product description says the agent confirms the item with the user and the wallet asks the user to approve the spend. Reuters also reported the app was initially offered to U.S. Pro and Max subscribers through a waitlisted research preview.

That is a specific product and an early availability description—not proof that every AI agent can safely or independently manage a company's purchasing. For businesses considering **AI agents for business purchasing**, the more useful lesson is to treat procurement as a controlled workflow, not a single “buy this” prompt. The agent may help prepare and coordinate a transaction, while a person retains the authority to confirm the item, price, and payment.

## What an AI purchasing workflow could help organize

Routine purchasing involves several small steps: identifying a need, comparing the requested item with purchasing rules, confirming the supplier and price, obtaining approval, placing an order, and keeping a record. A well-scoped agent workflow could assist with information gathering and task coordination, subject to the tools it actually has access to and the safeguards built around it.

Useful candidates for an initial workflow might include:

- Collecting a purchase request and the details needed for review.
- Checking that a requested item matches the description and quantity supplied by the requester.
- Preparing a comparison of available options for a human to assess.
- Routing the request to the right approver under an agreed internal process.
- Summarizing what was requested, what was approved, and what remains to be done.

These are workflow design ideas, not a claim that any named agent already performs every step. Capabilities depend on the specific product, connected services, permissions, and business process. Keep that distinction clear when evaluating a demo or product announcement.

## Design an approval-first process

Start with a written process that distinguishes what the agent may prepare from what requires a person's decision. A compact purchasing workflow can use these stages:

1. **Request:** Capture the requester, business purpose, item or service, quantity, preferred supplier, and delivery requirements.
2. **Validate:** Check the request against the details the business has actually provided, such as an approved supplier list or a budget rule. If a rule or record is missing, flag the gap instead of guessing.
3. **Prepare:** Present the proposed item, total cost, shipping or recurring terms if available, and any alternatives for review.
4. **Approve:** Ask the authorized person to confirm the exact purchase and amount before payment or order placement.
5. **Record:** Save the request and approval in a place the business can review later, including any changes made during the process.
6. **Follow up:** Report the order status or identify the next step, such as a delivery confirmation or invoice check, if the relevant system supports it.

The key is that approval attaches to a specific proposal. If the item, quantity, supplier, or price changes after the person reviews it, ask for confirmation again. This makes the boundary between preparation and authorization understandable to employees.

## Set permissions and limits before connecting tools

An agent can only act through the permissions and integrations available to it, so teams should decide the access they want before connecting purchasing, email, or payment services. Start with the smallest useful scope. For an early pilot, that might mean letting the agent read a request or prepare a summary while a person performs the final checkout.

Before enabling additional actions, write down:

- Which accounts and data the agent can access.
- Which actions it may perform without approval, if any.
- Which purchases always require a human decision.
- Who may approve a request, and how a substitute approver is handled.
- What happens when price, supplier, delivery terms, or item details do not match the request.
- Where the team can review an activity record or troubleshoot a failed step.

Do not rely on vague rules such as “buy approved things.” Define what “approved” means in the actual workflow and how the agent should behave when information is incomplete. Keep credentials and payment authorization separate from conversational instructions wherever the product supports those controls. An instruction in a chat should not be treated as a replacement for a business's established purchasing authority.

## Test one narrow, repeatable use case

Avoid beginning with every category of company spend. Choose one low-complexity purchasing task with a clear requester, a small number of acceptable options, and a known human approver. Run the process in a mode where an employee checks the agent's proposal before anything is ordered. Keep a manual path available for exceptions.

During the pilot, review examples of successful requests and exceptions. Ask whether the agent consistently captures the right details, whether reviewers can understand its recommendation, and whether any data is missing or ambiguous. Check how it handles changes and whether the person can stop or revise a request before authorization. These are operational questions to test, not outcomes that should be assumed from a product description.

If the workflow is unclear to employees, refine the intake form or approval steps before expanding permissions. If it cannot reliably distinguish an approved option from a merely similar one, keep the final selection with a person. The purpose of a pilot is to expose where the process needs better data or tighter rules—not to measure success by how many steps have been handed to software.

## Measure whether the workflow is useful

A business can evaluate a purchasing automation pilot using measures that reflect its own goals and baseline. For example, track how often requests arrive complete, how much employee review a proposal requires, whether approvals are easy to audit, and how frequently a person must correct or stop the process. Compare similar requests over a defined period, and record changes to policies or suppliers that could affect the result.

Pair any efficiency measure with a control check. A quicker workflow is not an improvement if employees cannot verify what was purchased or if approval becomes ambiguous. Review a sample of completed requests with the people responsible for purchasing and accounts payable. Note errors, exceptions, and missing evidence, then decide whether to adjust the workflow, keep the current scope, or pause it.

Do not promise a specific time or cost reduction without evidence from your own process. Results depend on request volume, supplier complexity, system integrations, approval requirements, and how well the rules are maintained.

## What to ask when evaluating an AI procurement agent

When comparing tools or planning an implementation, ask vendors and internal stakeholders concrete questions:

- Can the agent show the exact purchase proposal before an order is placed?
- Does approval cover the item and price, and what happens if either changes?
- Which connected accounts or payment methods are involved?
- Can the business limit access and distinguish a draft from an authorized transaction?
- What activity history is available for employees and reviewers?
- How does the system signal uncertainty, missing information, or a failed action?
- Can staff switch to the normal purchasing process without losing the request details?

Seek answers in the product's current documentation and test them in a controlled environment. Product announcements describe intended capabilities, but a business should verify the actual controls and limitations that apply to its account and region before relying on them.

## FAQ: AI agents for business purchasing

### Can an AI agent buy supplies for a business?

Some products are beginning to support purchasing-related tasks. Reuters reported that Claude Duet can make purchases via Stripe's Link wallet and Shopify Shop Pay, with user approval for sensitive actions. Capabilities and availability vary by product, so verify current terms and controls.

### Should an AI agent have authority to make purchases without review?

That depends on a business's risk tolerance and controls. A cautious starting point is to require a person to review the exact item, amount, and supplier before a purchase is authorized.

### What is an approval-first workflow?

It separates preparation from authorization: the agent can gather or organize information, but an authorized person confirms the specific proposed transaction before it proceeds.

### How should a company start using AI procurement automation?

Pilot one narrow, repeatable use case with limited permissions, a clear approval path, a manual fallback, and a review of both process quality and control exceptions.
