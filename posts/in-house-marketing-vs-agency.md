## The short answer: agency is usually cheaper until marketing becomes a full-time job

For most small and mid-sized businesses, an agency costs less than an in-house hire when the marketing budget is under roughly $150,000 a year in total. A single full-time marketing hire in the US typically costs well over $100,000 a year once salary, benefits and tools are counted, and that person still cannot cover ads, SEO, design, email and reporting on their own.

An agency retainer for a small business commonly lands between about $2,000 and $10,000 a month, depending on scope. That gives you several specialists for the price of one employee. The picture flips when you have enough work to keep a full-time person busy every week, usually when ad spend and campaigns are large and constant.

Treat the numbers in this post as planning ranges. Prices vary by city, by service and by how much work you hand over. Where a figure comes from a published source, it is named.

## What an in-house marketer really costs

Salary is only the start. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS) data on marketing managers, as reported by the salary site Hakia, puts the median at **$166,790 a year (May 2025)**, with the lowest-paid tenth under $90,260. That is a manager. A junior marketing coordinator or specialist costs less, and a hire in California usually costs more than the national figure.

On top of pay, plan for these costs:

- **Payroll taxes and benefits.** Health insurance, employer payroll tax, paid leave and retirement contributions commonly add a quarter to a third on top of salary. Ask your accountant for your actual figure.
- **Software.** Email platform, CRM, analytics, design tools, SEO tools and ad reporting often run several hundred dollars a month combined.
- **Recruiting.** Job ads, your time interviewing, and sometimes a recruiter fee.
- **Ramp-up time.** Expect two to three months before a new hire produces results you can measure.
- **Ad spend.** This is separate from either route and is paid to Google or Meta regardless.

As a worked example, using assumptions rather than a survey: a $75,000 specialist with a 25% load for taxes and benefits costs about $94,000. Add $6,000 a year for tools and you are near $100,000 before a single ad runs.

## What an agency really costs

Agencies charge in a few ways. A monthly retainer is the most common. Some charge a percentage of ad spend, and some price projects one at a time.

Typical planning ranges for a small business in the US:

- **Single channel, such as Google Ads or local SEO:** roughly $1,500 to $5,000 a month.
- **Two or three channels managed together:** roughly $4,000 to $10,000 a month.
- **Full-service with content, email and reporting:** $10,000 a month and up.

Our separate guides on [what a marketing agency costs in California](https://www.zapplon.com/services) and Google Ads management pricing go into the detail by channel. Here the point is the comparison: a $5,000 monthly retainer is $60,000 a year, and it can include a strategist, an ads specialist, a copywriter and a designer.

Watch for add-ons. Setup fees, minimum contract terms of three to six months, and separate charges for creative or landing pages are common. Ask for a written list of what is included.

## Side-by-side: the first-year cost at three business sizes

These scenarios are illustrations built on the assumptions above. Replace them with your own quotes.

**Small business, under $2 million revenue (one location, a few staff).**
- In-house: one junior hire at roughly $95,000 to $105,000 fully loaded, with skills in one or two areas.
- Agency: $2,500 to $5,000 a month, or $30,000 to $60,000 a year, covering ads and local SEO.
- Likely winner: the agency, by a clear margin.

**Mid-sized business, $2 million to $10 million revenue (a few locations or a growing online store).**
- In-house: one marketing manager plus a freelancer for design and ads, around $140,000 to $200,000 a year.
- Agency: $5,000 to $10,000 a month, or $60,000 to $120,000 a year.
- Likely winner: the agency on cost, though a hybrid starts to make sense.

**Larger mid-sized business, $10 million or more with constant campaigns.**
- In-house: a manager and one or two specialists, roughly $250,000 to $400,000 a year.
- Agency: $10,000 to $25,000 a month for broad scope.
- Likely winner: depends. In-house becomes competitive because you are using staff time every day.

## Where in-house wins

Cost is not the only measure. An in-house marketer makes sense in a few clear situations.

- **Marketing is daily, constant work.** If you publish, send and test every week, a full-time person is fully used. An agency bills for the same hours.
- **Deep product knowledge matters.** Complex or technical products, regulated services and brand-sensitive work benefit from someone who sits in your meetings.
- **You need speed.** Someone down the hall can change an offer the same afternoon.
- **You want to own the skills.** Knowledge stays in your business when staff stay.

The risk is concentration. If your one marketer leaves, your marketing stops, and rehiring takes months. A single person is also rarely strong at paid ads, SEO, copy and design at once.

## Where an agency wins

An agency tends to be the better value when:

- **You need several skills part-time.** You get ads, SEO and creative without hiring each role.
- **You need results soon.** A working agency has systems and templates already built, so campaigns can start within weeks.
- **Your needs change.** You can scale spend up for a busy season, or cut back, without redundancy costs.
- **You lack someone to manage a marketer.** An in-house hire still needs direction. If you cannot judge their work, you may pay a salary for little output.

The limits are real too. An agency serves many clients and will not know your business as well as an employee. Quality varies widely, and some agencies hand your account to a junior after the sales pitch. Reporting can also be vague, which is why you should insist on numbers you can check, such as leads, calls and booked jobs.

## The hybrid option many owners overlook

You do not have to choose one. A common setup at this size is an **agency for the technical, specialist work** (ad management, SEO, tracking) and **one part-time or junior person in-house** for content, customer stories, photos and local relationships.

Another option is a **freelancer** for a single task, such as Google Ads. A good freelancer can cost less than an agency, but you carry the risk of illness, holidays or disappearance. For a business with one location and a modest budget, that trade-off is often acceptable.

## How to decide: five questions

Answer these honestly and the choice is usually clear.

1. **How much do you spend on marketing in total, including ads?** Under about $150,000 a year, lean agency or hybrid.
2. **How many channels do you actually need?** Three or more favours an agency, because no single hire covers them.
3. **Is there a full week of work every week?** If you cannot fill 40 hours, a full-time hire is wasted money.
4. **Who would manage the marketer or the agency?** Someone has to approve work and check results either way. Budget a few hours a week of your own time.
5. **Can you wait three months for results?** A new hire usually needs the ramp-up. An agency may shorten that, though it is not guaranteed.

Whatever you choose, set targets before you sign or hire. Cost per lead, cost per booked appointment and monthly leads are all measurable. Review them every month.

## What to watch out for with each route

**With an agency:**
- Long contracts with no exit. Prefer 30-day notice after an initial period.
- Reports that show clicks and impressions but not leads or revenue.
- You do not own your ad accounts. Insist that the accounts, analytics and website remain yours.
- Promises of guaranteed rankings or guaranteed returns. Nobody can guarantee these.

**With an in-house hire:**
- Hiring a generalist and expecting specialist results in every channel.
- No budget for tools or training.
- No plan for what happens if they leave. Keep logins and documents in company-owned accounts.

## FAQ

### Is it cheaper to hire a marketing manager or use an agency?

For most small businesses, an agency is cheaper. A marketing manager's median pay is $166,790 a year according to BLS figures, before benefits and tools, while many small-business agency retainers run $30,000 to $120,000 a year. The gap narrows when you have enough work for a full-time team.

### How much should a small business spend on marketing?

There is no single correct share. The right amount depends on your margins, how fast you want to grow and how competitive your local market is. Start with a figure you can afford to test for three to six months, then increase spend on channels where you can see paid leads turning into customers.

### Can I start with an agency and move in-house later?

Yes, and many businesses do. Keep ownership of your ad accounts, website and data from the start, ask for documented processes, and the handover to an in-house hire is far easier.
