## What Meta announced for small businesses

Meta announced **Muse for Small Business** on September 29, 2026, expanding its Muse agent with new skills and connectors. In [Meta’s announcement](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/09/introducing-muse-small-business/), the company says Muse can connect Instagram professional account analytics, Facebook Pages, and Meta ad accounts, as well as dozens of business tools for work involving a company’s brand, storefront, books, and customer records. Meta says the full list of connectors is available in Muse app settings and that more are planned. [Engadget’s launch coverage](https://www.engadget.com/2271498/meta-now-has-a-version-of-muse-just-for-small-businesses/) lists early connectors including Asana, Box, Canva, Dropbox, Figma, Intuit QuickBooks, Klaviyo, Notion, Shopify, Slack, Stripe, and Zoom.

That is a notable shift from an AI assistant that only responds in a chat window: the product is being positioned around connections to services a small business may already use. Meta says Muse can draw on business context such as what a company sells, its brand voice, and common customer questions. But **a list of integrations is not a complete feature specification**. Confirm which actions the agent can take in each connected service, what permissions are available, and which account types can use each connection before relying on it for live work. Meta also says Muse supports custom connectors for services that are not yet in its supported list.

Meta says Muse is free for most needs and offers subscription plans for people who want more, but its announcement does not specify numeric prices or the usage thresholds for the small-business skills and connectors. The announcement describes Muse as available in the U.S. and Canada; businesses elsewhere should check the app and current eligibility details before planning a rollout. Because the new skills are inside Muse, check the current app settings and plan terms rather than assuming a particular connector or price is available to every business.

## Muse for Small Business and Meta’s enterprise platform are distinct

Meta has also announced a broader enterprise initiative. [Business Today’s coverage](https://www.businesstoday.in/technology/news/story/meta-launches-enterprise-platform-appoints-mongodb-ceo-chirantan-desai-as-head-558449-2026-09-29) describes Meta Enterprise Platform as an effort to bring tools such as the Muse agent, Meta Business Agent, Muse API, and Muse Code to business and developer users over time.

For a small business evaluating Muse, this distinction matters. The new Muse offering is described as connecting an agent with popular work apps and professional Meta accounts. The broader enterprise platform is a separate company initiative with a wider technology stack. Do not assume that an enterprise feature, API, security setting, or availability promise applies to the small-business product unless Meta documents it for that product.

The practical question is not simply, “Does it connect to my apps?” It is: **What task can it perform, with what data, under whose permission, and how can a person review or reverse the result?** Those answers determine whether an integration is useful for a real workflow.

## Choose one narrow workflow before connecting accounts

A good first test is specific, repeatable, and easy for a person to check. Avoid beginning with an open-ended instruction such as “run my marketing” or “manage my business.” Instead, write down the job, the source information, the expected output, and what must remain under human control.

A small marketing team might evaluate whether an agent can help assemble a campaign status summary from approved sources, organize a content-planning task, or prepare a draft for review. These are **pilot ideas**, not confirmed Muse features; check the current product documentation to learn what each integration actually supports. If the service cannot perform the narrow task or expose suitable controls, choose a different pilot rather than stretching the workflow to fit a marketing claim.

Before connecting an app, document:

- The business outcome the workflow is meant to support.
- The specific information the agent needs—and information it should not access.
- Whether the connection is read-only or can also create, edit, publish, message, or spend.
- The person who reviews the output and approves any consequential action.
- A simple way to stop the workflow and revoke access if the test goes wrong.

This short brief creates a baseline for comparing the agent with the team’s current process. It also prevents a general-purpose assistant from receiving more access than a narrow experiment needs.

## Check permissions before connecting an ad account or social profile

A connection to a Meta ad account or professional profile can involve business-sensitive information. The product’s exact permission model should be checked before linking an account: **can the agent view information only, or can it change live settings or publish content?** Do not infer permissions from the name of an integration.

During an initial evaluation, use the narrowest access the product makes available. If there is no suitable limited-access option, keep the connection out of a production workflow until the business understands the scope and has a review process. In particular, require a named person to approve changes that could publish public-facing content, contact a customer, alter a campaign, or affect spend.

Also review the provider’s applicable terms and privacy information for connected data. Confirm who can access the integration, how access is removed when a staff member leaves, and whether the activity can be reviewed. These are standard vendor-evaluation questions; the announcement does not, by itself, answer them for a particular account or integration.

## Run a controlled pilot and keep a human checkpoint

A measured pilot helps a team distinguish a useful workflow from an impressive demo. Start with a small amount of non-sensitive work or historical information when possible. Ask the agent to produce one defined output, then have a person compare it with the source applications and note corrections.

Use a simple review loop:

1. **Prepare a test case.** Choose a task the team already knows how to complete and define what an acceptable result looks like.
2. **Limit the connected services.** Start with only the accounts needed for that task, after reviewing the actual access requested.
3. **Review every output.** Check names, dates, financial information, campaign details, and any claims before the result is shared or acted on.
4. **Keep consequential actions manual.** Until the workflow has been evaluated, have a person approve posts, messages, campaign edits, and spending decisions.
5. **Record errors and time spent reviewing.** A task that creates a quick draft but takes longer to verify may not be a good automation candidate.
6. **Stop and adjust when access is unclear.** Do not treat a successful demo as proof that a connected workflow is safe for every business account.

The goal is not to remove a human from every step. A useful AI agent can assist with bounded work while a responsible person retains control of decisions that affect customers, money, or public communications.

## Measure the outcome, not the number of connected apps

An integration count is not a success metric. Define a short scorecard before the pilot so the team can decide whether to continue, adjust, or disconnect. For each test, track whether the task was completed, how much editing or checking was required, whether access stayed within the intended scope, and whether a human had to intervene.

For a marketing workflow, keep operational quality separate from campaign performance. A prepared draft, content calendar, or status summary does not prove that a campaign generated sales. If the agent helps with an advertising task, evaluate campaign results using the business’s normal measurement process and retain a person’s approval over changes to live campaigns.

At the end of the pilot, ask:

- Did the agent have access only to the information required for this task?
- Could staff review what it produced and identify where the information came from?
- Were the output and any actions accurate enough to justify the review time?
- Is there a clear owner for permissions, ongoing checks, and access removal?
- Would the team be comfortable repeating the workflow on a real customer or live account?

A “no” is useful. It may mean the workflow needs narrower scope, clearer documentation, or should remain manual for now.
